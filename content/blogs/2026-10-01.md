The name on the books is not the name on the birth certificate. Eric Arthur Blair was born on 25 June 1903 in Motihari, in Bengal, where his father worked in the Opium Department of the Indian Civil Service. The family was the usual colonial middle: not rich, not nobody, and certain that a boy of that class went to a school that would hurt him and then to a job that would place him over other people. He did both. Then he spent the rest of his life writing against the arrangement, under a name he picked because it sounded like a plain Englishman. George Orwell. There is a river of that name in Suffolk. He had no romance about the river. He wanted a name that would not smell of the school or the Burma police.

The novels and the essays are one argument, repeated until his lungs gave out. He said it himself in 1946, in "Why I Write." Every serious line he had written since 1936 was written, directly or indirectly, against totalitarianism and for democratic socialism, as he understood it. People who want him as a mascot for the right tend to drop the second half. People who want him as a mascot for the left tend to drop the first, or to pretend the first was only about Hitler. He kept both halves on purpose. The life is how he got there.

The novel is a separate piece, [Reading Nineteen Eighty-Four](/blog/2026-09-30). This one is about the man.

## Burma

He did not go to university. He was a King's Scholar at Eton, which is a cheap brilliance the school awards and then forgets if you do not turn into the right sort of man afterward. In 1922 he sat the exams and joined the Indian Imperial Police. They sent him to Burma. He was nineteen.

Five years of that work was enough. He resigned in 1927, on leave in England, and did not go back. The essay that comes out of it, "Shooting an Elephant," is not a confession you can file as a diary entry. It is a made thing. The fact underneath it is the one he wanted kept: a white officer in a colonial town does what the crowd expects, including killing an animal he does not need to kill, because the role is the job and the job is a performance of power. He hated the performance from both sides. He hated being hated, and he hated the empire that had put him there to be hated. *Burmese Days*, the novel, is the same disgust with the club, the drink, the gossip, and the man who knows the whole thing is a racket and stays for the salary.

He did not become a saint of anti-imperialism by leaving. He became a writer who had done the job and would not dress it up. That refusal is the start of the style. Concrete, slightly disgusted, unwilling to let a general word stand where a particular one will do.

## The bottom of the wage scale

Back in Europe he chose, for a while, to be poor on purpose, which is a different thing from being poor with no choice, and he knew the difference. He washed dishes in Paris. He went on the tramp in England, through the casual wards, the spikes, the hops in Kent. *Down and Out in Paris and London*, published in 1933, is that material. It is the first book under the new name. Victor Gollancz published it. Blair stayed the legal name for the rest of his life.

The point of the book is not tourism. He wanted the kind of knowledge you cannot get from a blue book. What a plongeur's week does to the legs. What a spike smells like. How charity is arranged so that the man receiving it is kept a little humiliated, because humiliation is part of the price. He trusted this sort of fact more than he trusted a theory that had not been in the room. The trust never left him. It is why his political writing is full of the price of a room, the taste of the food, the coal dust, and why he was rude to socialists who loved the working class in the abstract and disliked them in a railway carriage.

*The Road to Wigan Pier*, in 1937, is the test of that rudeness. Gollancz and the Left Book Club sent him to the industrial north to write about unemployment. He went to Wigan, Barnsley, Sheffield. He went down a mine, and the chapter on the crawl to the coal face is still the best thing in the book, because it does not generalize until the body has been accounted for. Then the second half turns on the audience that had ordered the book. He says the left is full of cranks, fruit-juice drinkers, nudists, and people whose socialism is a dislike of the ordinary. The Club printed the book with a preface that argued with him. He had done the job they asked and then refused to flatter the people who asked it. That refusal is more like him than any poster extracted from *Animal Farm*.

## Spain

In December 1936 he went to Spain to fight, not to report. He joined the militia of the POUM, a small party hated by the Soviet-line communists, because that was the unit his Independent Labour Party contacts could put him in. He was tall, the trenches were dug for shorter men, and he was shot through the throat at Huesca in May 1937. Before that he had been in Barcelona when the street fighting started. He saw who was on which barricade. Then he saw the newspapers, including papers of the wider left, describe the fighting as a fascist plot that had not happened the way he had just watched it happen.

The POUM was suppressed. He and his wife, Eileen O'Shaughnessy, whom he had married in June 1936, got out. *Homage to Catalonia*, 1938, is the account, and it sold badly. It is the book that makes the later novels inevitable. Not because Spain gave him a theory of totalitarianism out of a pamphlet. Because it gave him a specific experience of a record being changed while the witnesses were still in the city, and of his own side doing the changing. He came home with the wound, the TB that would worsen, and a hatred of the communist intellectual that was not a hatred of the worker and not a conversion to the right. He remained a socialist. He stopped believing that loyalty to a party was a reason to lie about a street.

Eileen matters in this and then disappears from the popular version of him, which prefers the solitary prophet. She was his reader. She kept the household going when he was ill or away, including during the war, when she was working in the censorship department and he was trying to be a writer and a soldier at once. Their son, Richard, was adopted in 1944. Eileen died in March 1945, on the operating table during what was supposed to be a routine surgery. Orwell was in Europe as a correspondent. He did not pretend, afterward, that he had been a good husband in the ordinary sense. The work is not an excuse. It is just the case.

## The war, the essays, the pigs

The army would not have him at the start of the war. His lungs were already bad. He joined the Home Guard, which he took more seriously than the joke about it, and from 1941 to 1943 he produced cultural talks at the BBC for broadcast to India. He was making propaganda for an empire he had already rejected, aimed at people the empire ruled, in a war he believed had to be fought. The contradiction is the interesting part, not a stain to be cleaned off. He did the work, disliked the institution, and left. The dreariness of that building, the clever people doing a mechanical job, goes into the Ministry of Truth later. He also learned, if he needed to learn it again, that a true war can still be described in rotten language.

The essays of these years are the plain statement the novels assume. "Politics and the English Language" is a manual against the sentence that hides the act. Dying metaphors, ready-made phrases, abstract nouns where a verb should be. Political language, as he describes it, is built to make lies sound truthful and murder respectable. "Notes on Nationalism" separates love of a place from the habit of identifying yourself with one unit so completely that the rest of the world exists only as its glory or its humiliation. He thought that habit was not confined to flags. A party, a church, a class could have it. "The Lion and the Unicorn" is the wartime pamphlet in which he tries to say what about England is worth keeping and what about its rulers is not. England, in his line, is a family with the wrong members in control. He wanted the wrong members out. He did not want the family abolished by a theory.

*Animal Farm* was finished in 1944 and almost not published. It is a short book about a revolution that keeps the slogans and changes the beneficiaries. The pigs can read. The other animals cannot quite remember the original wording on the barn wall. By the last page the pigs and the farmers are indistinguishable. Gollancz, his old publisher, refused it. Jonathan Cape accepted it and then dropped it after a word from the Ministry of Information. Faber declined. The Soviet Union was an ally, Stalingrad was recent, and a fable in which the revolutionary leaders become the farmers was treated as bad timing. Secker & Warburg published it in 1945, after the war in Europe was over. It made his name. It also fixed a misreading he would have to fight until he died: that the man who wrote it had left the left. He had left the willingness to lie for the left. Those are not the same departure.

The man at the Ministry who had leaned on Cape, Peter Smollett, was later shown to have been a Soviet agent. Orwell did not have the file. He had the suspicion, and he had the experience of watching *Animal Farm* stall in the offices of people who thought the ally must not be laughed at. That sequence matters when you get to the list.

## The list

In March 1949 Celia Kirwan, a friend, visited him at the sanatorium in Cranham. He was very ill. She had gone to work for the Information Research Department, a Foreign Office unit the Labour government had set up to argue against Stalinism. She wanted names of writers who might write for it. He was too sick to write for it himself. He sent her, on 2 May 1949, a list of about thirty-eight people he thought were crypto-communists, fellow-travellers, or inclined that way, and who therefore should not be trusted as anti-communist propagandists.

He had been keeping a notebook of such names already. It was not drawn up because a ministry asked. The notebook is longer, on the order of 135 names, with crossings-out where he changed his mind. The list he actually sent is the short one. Some entries are questions rather than verdicts. Chaplin is on it with doubt attached. The instruction he gave her was that it was libelous and not for the open. The practical effect he intended was narrow. These people should not be hired to write IRD pamphlets. They were not being marked for prison. A friend of his said later, in plain irritation at the legend, that nobody was going to be shot at dawn. They were not going to be asked to write.

He also told her that if anyone had kept such a list earlier, a man like Smollett would not have sat in a propaganda job long enough to do harm. On Smollett he was right. On some of the others he was scoring private grudges and secondhand reputations, and a few of the remarks are merely unpleasant. Both things are true. The list is not a secret-police warrant, and it is not nothing. A dying man who had written a whole career against the misuse of files still kept a file, and he gave part of it to a department of the state. He did it because he thought Stalin's admirers were already inside the institutions that explained the world, and because he had watched them protect the lie. He did it with the coldness of someone who no longer believed that "our side" was a reason to skip the check. If you want the uncomfortable reading, that is it. The comfortable one, that the list is a forgery or a betrayal that cancels the books, does not survive the dates.

## Jura

He had wanted out of London. In 1946 he went to Barnhill, a farmhouse at the north end of Jura, in the Hebrides, and he wrote the last novel there in conditions that were stupid for a man with his lungs. Damp, distance, a motorcycle accident that nearly killed him and the child, and the work. Eileen was already dead. He was looked after, unevenly, by his sister and by Richard's presence, and he typed while he could. The book was finished at the end of 1948. In 1949 he was in hospitals. On 13 October 1949, in a hospital room, he married Sonia Brownell. He died on 21 January 1950. He was forty-six.

Sonia became the widow who controlled the estate, which is a story about publishing and not about the sentences. The sentences were done. *Animal Farm* and *Nineteen Eighty-Four* are the two books that carried the name, and they are both narrower than the reputation. One is about a revolution eating itself. The other is about a Party that owns the record, the language, and at last the arithmetic. Neither one is a letter of resignation from the left. He had already said which socialism he meant. It was the kind that did not require you to look at a street you had stood on and agree it had been a different street.

## What he kept

The habits are more useful than the portrait of the saint or the informer.

He distrusted a general idea that had not been tested against a particular body, wage, room, or battle. When the idea survived the contact, he kept it. When it didn't, he insulted the people still holding it, including his friends.

He thought language was part of the crime, not the decoration on top of it. A soft noun for a killing was not a style problem. It was how a killing gets into a minute without anyone present having to picture it. The cure he offered is unspectacular. Say what happened. Use the short word. Keep a copy the speaker does not control.

He would not treat an ally as a reason. The Soviet Union could be necessary to the defeat of Hitler and still be a lie-machine. The empire could be the thing that employed his father and still be a racket. Labour could be his government and still be wrong about a book. The moment "our side" settles a question of fact, you are already in the department he gave Winston a job in.

And he was ordinary in the ways that annoy the legend. A bad husband for long stretches. A snob who tried to argue himself out of the snobbery and only partly managed it. A man who kept a list. A smoker with ruined lungs who went to live in a wet house because he wanted a garden and a novel. The work is not canceled by any of that, and it is not improved by hiding it. He asked to be read as a writer who had picked a side, democratic socialism, and then spent twenty years trying to stop that side from lying. The books are what it looked like when he thought he was failing, and when he thought the failure had a shape you could draw in a fable, and then in a city where the past is edited by the morning shift.
