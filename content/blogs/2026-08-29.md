# The Digital Renaissance Man: Why Polymaths Win in the AI Age

There will be three types of people in the future. The gap between them keeps widening.

If you want to come out ahead over the next five to ten years, it's essential that you don't fall into group one or two.

## 1) The Purists

"We're the ones who stayed clean."

These are the people who rejected AI entirely and retreated into nature, simplicity, and a screen-free existence. It looks peaceful, until they run into someone using any form of technology, at which point they react with surprising hostility, because that person threatens the identity they've built around this new belief system.

Watching a Purist, it's genuinely hard to tell whether they're sincere or just performing what currently reads as rebellious and cool.

## 2) The Automatons

These are the people who outsource every decision they make. They can't tolerate friction and are hooked on optimization. If their eyes aren't on a screen, they feel like they're falling behind in some invisible race. Every thought passes through an AI model first.

They can no longer tell where the AI ends and their own life begins.

Most people assume you have to pick one of these two camps. I happen to enjoy going against that assumption. Maybe that's a flaw in my character, maybe it comes off as try hard, but there's real satisfaction in it for me. Living in a way that almost nobody else does feels like a creative challenge, and in my experience, real growth (and the meaning that comes with it) never comes from following the crowd.

I think both camps are wrong.

The Purists write off an entire dimension of reality (external progress), treat it as something evil, and assume it's impossible to integrate it in a healthy way through prioritization and intentional effort.

![img1](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HQwCydQbgAArHxv?format=png&name=large)

Reality has four quadrants. Think of it like a skill tree in a video game. You invest points into each one as you gain experience and level up, and they're all connected. You can pour everything into money, but if your mind, body, and spirit haven't developed at all, money will only take you so far. The real rewards in life go to people willing to keep developing across every one of these domains.

Purists elevate their internal world (mind and spirit) as the only truth that matters and dismiss the external world entirely.

That's a setup for disaster. It doesn't matter how enlightened you feel. If your body is weak, your health is compromised, and you have no money to your name, you will suffer needlessly, and because you've labeled the solution to your problems as evil, you're left with no option but to play the victim.

The Automatons go the opposite direction. They fixate on money and progress so relentlessly that their humanity gets buried entirely.

There is a third path, though. It sounds absurd and unrealistic to people who can't think independently, but the future belongs to those who become multi-dimensional.

Here's how you win over the next decade.

## We're in a Second Renaissance

Proof of work is replacing the resume. An audience is replacing the employer. Taste is replacing credentials.

The great tragedy of our era is that we were convinced the Mechanical Life was the only route to success. You were told to pick one lane. You were made to believe that having wide-ranging interests was a flaw. Become a designer. Become a writer. Become an engineer, a marketer, a salesperson, or just grab whatever pays well, because that's supposedly how you earn respect and live a good life.

That advice seemed reasonable at the time, but now it's left you feeling replaceable, because none of it was ever how humans were actually meant to live.

The modern concept of a "job" is only about 250 years old. Before that, the ideal was a free person who did many different things over the course of a life, in contrast to a person bound to one narrow, specialized task (sounds familiar). The artisan, the farmer, the pirate, these were people who could run an entire operation, not just handle one small piece of it. They were thinker and maker, artist and scientist at once, and they understood that a good life couldn't be confined to a single field of study.

Leonardo da Vinci walked into Verrocchio's workshop to learn painting and walked out understanding anatomy, engineering, optics, and bronze casting. Nobody told him these subjects had to be separated into distinct credentials or degrees.

During the Renaissance, nobody cared about your niche. They cared about what you could actually do.

Industrialization's factory model was never designed to wipe out education, critical thinking, individual agency, and taste, but that's exactly what happened. It pulled us away from the Renaissance ideal. Education turned into training rather than discovery. Repetitive employment kept us disconnected from the entrepreneurial process, so we never built the experience or agency to create something of our own.

Unfortunately, this has become our default. The linear career instead of the exponential one. Most people never even question it.

Now that AI has arrived, only people with an open mind, who don't fully identify with either camp rejecting or blindly embracing this next phase, will actually capitalize on the shift.

One person can now accomplish what used to take a team of ten to fifty people, depending on the task. One person has instant access to essentially all known information. One person has free distribution for their work through social media.

If you're a genuine New Renaissance Man, this is the best possible time to be alive, even as plenty of people try to convince you the world is falling apart.

AI can now absorb the mechanical, repetitive tasks that defined our way of life for the past two centuries. People worry AI will dull our thinking and turn us into machines, without noticing they've already been living like machines their entire lives.

If you're joining this Second Renaissance, your job is to pick your craft, use AI to manage the full operation, and focus your energy on taste, direction, and judgment, because AI output has no perspective of its own. It's artificial intelligence in the most literal sense, more like a bag of water waiting to be shaped.

## Become a Multi-Dimensional, Irreplaceable, Walking Contradiction

The most compelling people are walking contradictions. Jacked intellectuals. Casual billionaires. Spiritual warriors. Life is far more interesting when you can't be neatly filed away into a box everyone else understands.

Going forward, the most interesting people will live between two extremes: pure creativity and pure productivity. They'll move effortlessly between an analog lifestyle (no screens, no urgency, genuine enjoyment of life) and an AI native lifestyle (using technology to accomplish what was previously impossible).

Meaning and fulfillment don't come from a flat, evenly "balanced" life. They come from contrast. If you were happy constantly, you wouldn't even register it as happiness, you'd just call it normal, maybe even a little dull, and you'd need something even more intense to feel what you currently think of as happiness (this is partly why people can't put their phones down; an ordinary happy life feels too boring). But when you struggle and fail at something you genuinely care about, you learn what real happiness actually feels like on the other side.

The people who win will lean into that contrast. They work hard and rest deeply. Intense, focused bursts on their craft and life's work, followed by real periods of rest, the same rhythm you see in the world's most accomplished creatives.

Charles Darwin published 19 books in his lifetime. His daily routine would surprise most people given that output:

- 7:00 AM: A short walk, then breakfast.
- 8:00 to 9:30 AM: His first and most productive work session in the study.
- 9:30 to 10:30 AM: A break to read letters and spend time with family.
- 10:30 AM to noon: A second work session. By this point Darwin considered himself to have "done a good day's work."
- After noon: A second walk, this time along the Sandwalk, a quarter mile gravel path he built specifically for thinking.
- After lunch: Reading the newspaper, then resting while his wife read to him aloud.
- 4:00 PM: A third walk, often on the Sandwalk again, sometimes with company.

Darwin logged roughly three hours of focused, creative work each day, split across two sessions.

I think the people who thrive going forward will follow a similar rhythm, but with the output of ten to twenty people, capable of single handedly building their life's work. They'll run what I call a One Human Business (a concept I'll break down separately).

> "There are almost 7B people on this planet. Someday, I hope, there will be almost 7B companies." — Naval Ravikant

Before factories and industrialization, you worked until the task was finished, not until an eight hour shift ended. Your schedule followed the seasons, sunlight, and your own energy. Just as important, you didn't learn how to become valuable through a standardized curriculum and meaningless tests. You learned by doing, moving from apprentice to journeyman to master in a workshop, copying someone who already knew what they were doing until your own taste and judgment took shape.

So the real question becomes: how do you actually become a New Renaissance Man?

## How to Become a Digital Renaissance Man

Do it all. Become a writer, a designer, a marketer, a filmmaker, a bodybuilder, a runner, whatever your curiosity pulls you toward. But direct all of that focus and energy into one vessel. One craft. One thing you choose to be known for, with everything else you've developed feeding into your ability to outpace everyone around you.

Here's the approach.

### Choose Your Craft, Become Irreplaceable

Most artists, writers, and creatives are currently up in arms about AI because they've tied their identity to a specific skill, tool, or way of working.

I'm not interested in debating whether AI art or AI writing counts as "real" art or writing. That debate is a convenient distraction that keeps people from actually building anything with their lives, aside from complaining for engagement and reinforcing an identity that won't serve them well going forward.

You need a craft, but you need to choose it deliberately. Your craft sits at the intersection of what you genuinely care about, what you're uniquely capable of, and what people are actually willing to pay for.

There are three layers to building a truly irreplaceable skill set.

**Layer 1 is a foundation in human nature.** Marketing, sales, writing, speaking, psychology, and similar disciplines. It's about understanding how attention actually works and how perceived value is deeply subjective and shifts as the world changes. Anyone who wants to succeed independently, in any form, has to master this layer. This is the "what people are willing to pay you for" piece.

**Layer 2 is your specific obsession and unique interests.** Philosophy, fitness, design, coding, nutrition, or anything else. None of these fields are disappearing, they're simply evolving. Your uniqueness comes from how you combine them. The philosopher who's also physically strong is far more compelling and valuable than the one who only develops their mind without ever testing their ideas against real experience. People gravitate toward you instead of someone else. This is the "what you care about" and "what you can uniquely do" piece, and it's what draws people to you specifically. The human nature foundation is what makes that appealing to them.

**Layer 3 is technical skill.** Email marketing platforms, Photoshop, no-code website builders, social media management, writing raw code. This used to matter most, because these tools took real time and effort to learn. Now AI handles most of it effortlessly, so it's a mistake to bet your future purely on mastering a tool.

Every technological shift follows the same pattern: the mechanical portion of a skill gets automated, and the human role shifts up a level. The scribe became the editor. The hand weaver became the machine operator.

### How to Become Dangerously Self Educated

Schools teach you how to function within society. That's fine, and to some extent necessary, but it doesn't teach you how to chart your own path. Public education functions more as a filtering and conditioning system than anything else.

Real education is discovery. It's the process of learning exactly what you need to pursue goals and a vision you set for yourself, not hoarding knowledge or memorizing facts for tests disconnected from reality.

Worse, most people stop learning entirely once they graduate. They hit twenty or twenty five and repeat the same day on loop for the rest of their lives, never chasing a creative challenge demanding new knowledge.

So how do you actually learn? You follow the apprenticeship model. The great thing about today is that you can find mentors and masters just by opening a book or pulling up YouTube on your phone.

I don't care if you use AI to gather information. I don't care if you get all your advice from a single YouTube personality. What matters is whether you have a vision and a goal, work toward it, and apply what you learn to real problems as they come up. That's how you actually verify whether the information holds up for your specific situation.

Whether or not AI hallucinates, there are still plenty of genuinely poor sources of human advice you probably shouldn't trust either way. You have to question, test, and experiment regardless of where the information comes from.

Don't learn first and act later, that's the fastest route into tutorial hell. Attempt the thing first, or try to, and only then is your mind actually ready to absorb the lesson that moves you forward.

### The One Human Business (and Joining the New Economy)

Creators are replacing institutions. I know that sounds like an exaggeration, but look around.

Creators are the new education system: aren't most of the genuinely useful things you know these days coming from Substack, YouTube, podcasts, or niche accounts on X?

Creators shape culture, and culture shapes behavior: don't you trust a creator whose thinking you've followed closely more than a politician who clearly has their own agenda?

The internet offers permissionless leverage: you don't need anyone's approval to build an audience, launch a business, or sell a product. It's the highest agency path available.

AI accelerates the generalist and the polymath: you can now run a one person business, using content to build an audience around your work, code to build the product, funnel, and digital presence, and deep domain knowledge to make what you offer more valuable than the next person's.

When I say "creator," you probably picture an influencer or a personal brand. That's not quite what I mean. I mean someone who genuinely creates value, solves problems, builds solutions, and helps others do the same. Since value exchange is the foundation of every human relationship, people return that value, often in the form of money.

James Clear does have a personal brand, sure, but really he's using newsletters, books, and content to put his life's work in front of people and earn an independent income.

I think most ambitious people will eventually take this route, regardless of whether today's social media platforms remain the delivery mechanism. In the past it was newspapers and radio. Today it's social media. Who knows what it'll be next, but starting there right now puts you ahead of most people, since that's where attention currently lives.

A personal brand is simply the highest leverage vehicle for being a creator at this moment. It's a shame the term has been reduced to a marketable "content creator" identity, but that's the current landscape. That doesn't mean you have to conform to whatever you imagine a content creator is supposed to look like.

### How to Achieve the Impossible with AI

Most people don't realize AI is more than a slop generator or a fancier search engine. They don't grasp that they can literally ask it to build software, a landing page, a funnel, an app, or nearly anything else they need on the internet.

"But that just produces AI slop!"

Correct, and that's exactly where most people stop. They don't realize iteration is the whole game, and that their first attempt would be rough with or without AI involved.

A landing page starts as a rough draft, and it's on you to refine it through small adjustments until it actually converts. The software is buggy and unpolished at first, until you fix those bugs repeatedly until it outperforms nearly everything else on the market.

In short, most people don't understand that they can simply ask "how do I do this?" and keep asking follow up questions until a clear plan forms, then direct the AI to execute a vision you've defined and control, rather than accepting some random first output, and keep iterating until it's genuinely valuable.

It still takes real time, and anyone chasing a quick dopamine hit will burn out fast.

Here's a quick start guide for doing anything with AI:

**1) Know what you want and what "good" actually looks like.**
Most people will use AI to build a one person business. If you don't already understand how to build a business, you'll expect AI to somehow do it flawlessly without you knowing what you actually need. It still takes years of iteration to see meaningful results, that part hasn't changed. But once you know what you want, what you need, and what a good outcome looks like, you can simply ask AI how it can help you get there. The best starting point is treating AI as a learning tool. Offer creation, copywriting, social strategy, whatever it is, AI can't help with something you don't even think to ask about.

**2) Hold absurdly high standards.**
Every AI response feels like slop because you're treating the first draft as the finished product. You have to keep tweaking every design choice, feature, word, and sentence AI produces. What you build with AI ends up mediocre because you expect it to behave like a flawless genius, rather than treating it as a tool that matches your own intelligence and ambition.

**3) Try to replace yourself with AI.**
The fastest way to actually learn AI is to attempt to automate everything you currently do. You'll quickly discover what can and can't be automated, and only then can you decide which parts of your business and craft you actually want AI touching.

Personally, AI is clearly excellent at scanning large volumes of information for patterns and trends. It would be foolish to manually research top performing YouTube videos, tweets, and articles by hand now, that puts you at a real disadvantage, so I let AI handle that research.

But when it comes to writing the actual posts, I keep that manual for two reasons. First, I enjoy the creative process of writing and treat it as part of my day. Second, the value of writing lives entirely in the reader's experience of it. With code, the end user never sees or cares what the code looks like, only whether it works for them. Writing is different, which is why I write it myself, for precise control over the value I'm delivering.

If you're not sure where to start, think of anything you do daily, then ask AI how that task could become a repeatable process through a prompt, skill, tool, or piece of software. And remember, that automation is also just a first draft. It'll be rough initially. Just tell it how to improve.
