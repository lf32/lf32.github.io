A book can be finished and still not have been read. The pages moved. A mood arrived. The opinion you had in the first chapter is the opinion you leave with, now wearing two or three of the author's phrases. What happened in between was recognition. The book resembled something you already believed, or something you had been told to admire, and you stored the resemblance.

There is another way through the same pages. You treat the book as a structure that is standing on something, and you find the something. Those supports are first principles. For this job, a first principle is a proposition the rest of the argument needs and does not itself establish. You locate those propositions. You check whether the later pages follow from them. Then you build the argument again, in sentences you could say with the book closed. If you can, you have read it. If you cannot, you have been in the room with it.

The method is old. Aristotle, in the Posterior Analytics, describes a real demonstration as one that starts from things that are true, prior to the conclusion, and able to hold the conclusion up. Those starting points are the first principles. Euclid's Elements is that standard with the paint scraped off. A physics argument is the same standard with the principles named and the consequences added up in public. A modern book is rarely so clean. The reading is the same work, done on a messier object.

## What you are actually holding

A book is a sequence of sentences a person chose to put in that order. Around the sequence there is a reputation, a cover line, a syllabus, a friend who said it changed them. Those are other sentences, from other people. They belong in the room after you know what this sequence says. Brought in earlier, they do the reading for you, and they do it for their own purposes.

The parts that can hold weight are few.

The terms, in the sense this author gives them. Freedom, market, trauma, the people, proof. Import the sense you arrived with, and you are reading a book you wrote, with this one hired to supply examples.

The claims. A chapter that feels crowded usually has one claim and a crowd of illustrations. An illustration is the same claim in clothes. It is not a second claim.

The reasons. A reason is the step that is supposed to make the claim follow. "Studies show" is a gesture toward a place where a reason might be standing. The gesture is not the reason.

The evidence. A particular: a number, a date, an experiment, a document, a scene someone observed. Evidence has a size. The adverb placed in front of it leaves the size alone.

The scope. The line where the author says the claim stops, or the line where the author goes quiet and the claim keeps walking. Silence at the boundary is part of the structure. You record it as silence. You do not record it as permission to carry the claim into your own week.

Tone, insults, the memoir chapter, the rival dismantled in chapter nine: any of these can be holding the building up, and any of them can be decoration. You learn which by trying to stand the argument up after they have been set aside.

## Where the starting points are

A first principle is not a sentence you find noble. It is a sentence you are willing to take as basic for the length of this argument, because digging under it will not change your judgment of the book in front of you. "Unsupported bodies fall toward the earth" is basic enough on a page about a clock. It is the thing under investigation on a page of Galileo. The floor moves. The skill is knowing which floor this book is on.

A lot of reading never reaches a floor. It compares. This book resembles the last one that was good. This author stands on the side of a fight you already picked. This paragraph sounds like seriousness, and the claim inherits the sound. Comparison is a real instrument. Used first, it files the book before the book has been opened. You learn the shelf. The shelf is not the argument.

The practical test is short. Take the sentence the chapter seems proud of. Ask what would already have to be true for that sentence to be a conclusion rather than a wish. Write those earlier things down. If they are not in the book, the book is borrowing them from you. You may lend them. You should know you are lending them.

## A pass through one book

Do this on paper once, before you decide what you think.

Say what the book is for, in one sentence a person could use who has never heard the title. "A history of the grain trade in the thirteenth century" is a sentence of that kind. "A powerful meditation on power" is a reputation. If you can only produce the reputation, read the table of contents as slowly as you meant to read the chapters, and try the sentence again.

Collect the terms the book cannot survive without. Five is already a lot. Ten is usually a list of topics. Beside each term, write the author's definition inside the author's boundaries. If the author never defines it, write that down. A load-bearing word with no definition is a hole in the floor. The hole is a finding.

Mark the claims the chapters exist to support. Then mark every story, case, and quotation as evidence or as illustration. Evidence is doing work: take it away and a support is gone. Illustration is keeping you company: take it away and the claim is the claim it was. This feels unfair to the author, which is a sign you are near the method. A chapter can move you and leave its claim as bare as it was on the first page.

For each claim, write the reason in plain order, with the adjectives gone. Where the reason is an authority, the authority is the reason, and you say so. Where the reason is a study, the study's limits are the claim's limits. Where the reason is "this is obvious," write that the author is treating the sentence as a first principle. Then decide, as a separate act, whether you will treat it that way too. A physics text does this in the open and calls the sentence a law. The exercises are the derivation. Most other books do it by tone of voice. The tone is not the sentence. Write the sentence down anyway.

Ask what would make the claim false on the book's own terms. A claim that cannot be wrong inside the rules the book wrote has been insulated. Insulation is a fair aim in a creed or a lyric. In a book that offers itself as an account of how something is, the insulation is the object you came to find.

Close the book. From the terms and the reasons, say the argument in a few sentences. Open it again. See what you inserted and what you dropped. Insertions are yours. Keep them if you label them as yours. Drops are either decoration or a step you did not follow. The next sitting is for those steps, not for a fresh tour of the sentences you already understood.

Other books come after the inventory. Resemblance can then show you that this argument repeats an older one, or answers it, or steals from it, or actually extends it. Before the inventory, resemblance is how the inventory gets skipped.

## The first proposition of Euclid

The clearest page for the method is the first proposition of Euclid's Elements. Almost nothing is concealed, and the one concealment is famous.

The job is to construct an equilateral triangle on a given finite straight line. Call the line AB. The tools already on the table are few. A straight line may be drawn from any point to any point. A finite straight line may be extended. A circle may be drawn with any center and any distance. All right angles are equal. The parallel postulate is also there, and this proposition does not use it. Beside the postulates are the common notions, the rules for handling equality, and the definitions. A circle, on his definition, is a plane figure in which every straight line from the center to the boundary is equal. The postulates, the common notions, and the definitions are the first principles of the proof. The equilateral triangle is not among them. It has to be built.

The construction runs like this. Draw the circle with center A and radius AB. Draw the circle with center B and radius BA. Take a point C where the two circles cut one another. Join C to A and C to B. AC equals AB, because both are lines from the center of the first circle to its boundary. BC equals BA for the same reason in the second circle. Things equal to the same thing are equal to one another, which is his first common notion, so AC equals BC. The three sides match. The triangle is equilateral, and it sits on the line you were given.

Read it as a building and one stone was never listed. Nothing he has postulated says that those two circles must meet. The diagram shows them meeting. The diagram is not a postulate. A reader who simply sees the intersection has stepped off the listed foundations and onto the picture. Later axiom systems had to write down, as a principle, an assumption Euclid used and did not record. The triangle still comes out. The lesson is the note in the margin. Assumed, not given.

That note is the method. You owe it to Euclid, and you owe it to the paperback that explains a war, a market, or a life by two curves that "obviously" cross. The paperback is also hoping you will see the intersection because the drawing is confident.

## The same move in physics

Physics is where the phrase still has a job. A first principle is a proposition you grant at the start, so that the rest can be derived, and so that you can see when a page has stopped deriving and started hoping. A result is whatever follows. Mixing the two is the ordinary way of finishing a chapter without having read it. You remember the striking sentence. You cannot say which earlier sentence it stands on.

Galileo is the clean demolition of a false principle. The principle he inherited, from Aristotle, was that a heavier body falls faster than a lighter one. He does not need the Leaning Tower, and the tower story is a later decoration. Tie the heavy body to the light one. If weight sets the speed, the light body drags on the heavy one, and the pair falls more slowly than the heavy one would have fallen alone. The pair is also heavier than the heavy one alone, so the same principle says the pair falls faster. One first principle, two verdicts. The principle is finished. What replaces it is narrower than the sentence people remember. Set the resistance of the air aside, and the rate at which a falling body picks up speed does not depend on its weight. That is one principle. A second grant, separate from it, is that this picked-up speed grows steadily with time: equal gains of speed in equal times. From that second grant the distance fallen grows with the square of the time. The square is a result. It does not follow from the independence of weight, and the independence of weight does not follow from the square. He checked the square on an incline, where the motion is the same fall slowed down and a clock can keep up. The measurement is evidence for the result. It is not a further foundation. A page that asks you to memorize both sentences as a pair of "Galileo's laws" has hidden which one is a principle and which one is a theorem.

Newton writes the foundations as axioms and then sends them a bill. Three laws of motion. Then a law of attraction: two masses pull on each other in proportion to the product of the masses, and in inverse proportion to the square of the distance between them. Kepler had already extracted three regularities from the sky. Planets move in ellipses with the sun at one focus. A line from the sun to a planet sweeps equal areas in equal times. The square of the orbital period grows with the cube of the distance. Those were findings. In the Principia they are conclusions, and they are not even conclusions from the same part of the principle. Equal areas follow from any force aimed at the sun. The ellipse, and the tie between period and distance, follow from the inverse square in particular. The moon is that argument brought down to a distance you can state. The moon is about sixty earth-radii out. A pull that has the strength of ordinary weight at the surface, and that weakens with the square of distance, is weaker out there by a factor of about thirty-six hundred. The acceleration with which the moon falls away from a straight line agrees with that weakening. The falling apple is a story Newton told in old age. It is not a page of the derivation. If you can say "planets move in ellipses" and you cannot say which force makes the ellipse necessary, you have the result and you have missed the first principle.

Einstein's 1905 paper on moving bodies is two first principles and a long invoice. The laws of physics take the same form in every inertial frame, meaning every laboratory that moves at constant velocity. And the speed of light in empty space does not depend on how the source is moving. From those two grants, a stack of results follows. Events that are simultaneous for one observer need not be simultaneous for another. A clock moving relative to you runs slow by your clocks. A length along the direction of that motion comes out shorter by your rods. The equivalence of mass and energy is a further consequence, from a short paper the same year, and it is not one of the two grants. The sentence people promote into a principle is a different sentence, and it fights both of his. "Everything is relative." His first principle says the laws are the same in every inertial laboratory, which is a claim that something does not change when you change frames. His second says the speed of light does not depend on the motion of the source. Put the two together and every such laboratory measures that same speed. A reader who begins from the nickname has begun on an upper floor, and the floor is not even one the argument built.

The cut is the same outside mechanics.

Lavoisier's working principle is that, in a closed change, the mass you begin with is the mass you end with. A balanced chemical equation is that principle with the substances named. The principle it replaced said that a substance called phlogiston left a body when the body burned. That older principle organized a lot of flame, and a closed vessel plus a scale was enough to finish it: a metal heated in air can gain mass, which a departing substance does not do. When you read a chapter on a reaction, do Euclid's work on it. Find the sentence that is being used as conservation. Treat the flame as illustration. If the chapter's marvel only works because mass appears from nowhere, the marvel is the smuggled intersection again.

Thermodynamics is stricter, because the principle it is famous for is a ban. Kelvin's statement of the second law: no cyclic engine takes heat from a single source and turns all of it into work, with nothing else changed. Clausius's statement, which marks out the same boundary: heat does not of itself pass from a colder body to a hotter one. A proposed machine that would break either statement is done as an argument. You do not have to build it to know the books will not balance. The picture of entropy as "disorder" is an illustration that got promoted into the office of the law. It earns its keep for a moment on a gas mixing in a box. It is not the sentence you can apply to the next machine, and it will not warn you on a system the picture was never drawn for. Write Kelvin's sentence or Clausius's sentence as the first principle. Keep the mess as an example, and only on the days it is one.

A textbook sometimes does you the courtesy of putting the first principles in a box and the results in the exercises. A popular book of physics rarely does. The marvel is in the prologue. The law, if it appears, appears afterward as a comment on the marvel. Reverse the order when you take notes. Write the propositions you are being asked to grant. Write the result that is supposed to follow. Then read the middle once, looking only for the steps between those two lists. A result with no middle is a sentence you are being asked to admire. Admiration is available to someone who has not read the chapter. The derivation is the reading.

## When the book is not a proof

A novel, a memoir, a book of poems: the floor changes. The obligation does not.

The fundamentals are the features the book cannot lose without becoming a different book. What happens, and in what order. Who is in a position to know it. Which words return when the pressure rises. What is shown, and what is only said by a narrator who wants something. "What it is about" is a conclusion. You earn it after that inventory, in the way the triangle is earned after the postulates and the orbit after the force, or you do not earn it. A preface that announces the theme is another speaker. Read it after the pages, and check whether the pages built the thing the preface sold.

This is why a summary is so often a second book, smaller and worse. The summary keeps the billed theme and lets the order go, and the narrator's ignorance, and the repeated words. Those were the foundations. The theme was an upper floor. It is possible to live on the upper floor for years and to call the view reading.

## Notes and pace

Notes are the inventory. A paragraph copied into a notebook is a decision to read it later, stored under the sensation of having read it already. Copy the term and the definition you were given. Copy the claim in a clause of your own. Copy the evidence in a form that still has its size: the number, the date, the sample, or the fact that you were handed an anecdote where a record was required. Leave the author's prose in the book. It will still be there.

Pace is a consequence of knowing which sentences are foundations. Those sentences are a minority. Once they are visible, the illustrations can go by at the speed of recognition. Before they are visible, speed is a way of reaching the last page while still holding the first page's opinion. The test is dull and sufficient. Shut the book. Rebuild the argument. A person who can do that has read it. A person who can find the passage again has handled the object.

One chapter done this way will teach your hand more than a month of underlining. A line under a sentence records agreement. The inventory records a test.

## Where the reading goes wrong

The common failure is to adopt the author's last page as your first principle, and then read backward, pleased at how surely everything led there. It will lead there. The book was assembled in that direction, and your eye has joined the assembly. It is the mistake of memorizing the ellipse and calling it Newton's principle, or memorizing "everything is relative" and calling it Einstein's. The last page is a result. A result cannot hold itself up. The repair is the same move as the first one. Go back to the word that was never defined. Go back to the step that was a picture. Say the argument with the finishing adjective removed, and see what is still standing.

You will do this worst on the books you love. Affection is a kind of resemblance. It files the book among your belongings, and the inventory then feels like a discourtesy. The inventory is not a verdict. It is the condition for a verdict that is yours, rather than a verdict you are borrowing from the last page and calling a response.

A book that is still standing after you rebuild it is a tool you can pick up without the author's company. A book that is not standing has paid you in a different coin. You know which stone was a drawing. You can stop repeating the drawing as if it were a foundation. A large part of reading is that refusal, practiced until it becomes the way you turn a page.
