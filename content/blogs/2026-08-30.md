# 

If you're like me, you probably think New Year's resolutions are pretty pointless. Most people go about transforming their lives the wrong way entirely. They make resolutions because that's what everyone does chasing a shallow sense of meaning through social ritual, but these resolutions never touch the deeper mechanics that real change requires. It's not about hyping yourself up to be more "disciplined" or "productive" this year.

If that's been you, I'm not writing this to judge you (though I'll admit my writing tends to run blunt). I've abandoned far more goals than I've ever hit probably ten times as many. Honestly, I think that's true for most people. The fact that so many of us try to reinvent our lives and consistently fall short doesn't change, no matter how many years pass.

Still, even though I think resolutions themselves are silly, there's real value in taking stock of the parts of your life you can't stand, so you can propel yourself toward something far better. That's what we're getting into here.

So if you're aiming to launch a business, reshape your body, or finally commit to a more meaningful path without abandoning ship after two weeks, I've got seven ideas for you likely ones you haven't come across before — on behavior change, psychology, and productivity, so you can make 2026 different.

This is going to be thorough.

This isn't the kind of piece you skim and forget five minutes later.

This is something worth bookmarking, annotating, and setting time aside to really sit with.

The exercise at the end designed to help you dig into your own psyche and figure out what you actually want takes roughly a full day to work through, but its impact stretches far beyond that single day.

Let's get into it.

## I – You're Not Where You Want to Be Because You're Not Yet the Person Who Belongs There

When people set ambitious goals, they usually focus on one of two levers:

- Changing their actions to inch closer to the goal (the less important, secondary lever)
- Changing their identity so the right actions happen naturally (the far more important, primary lever)

Most people pick a shallow goal, psych themselves up to stay disciplined for a few weeks, and then quietly slide back into old habits — because they were building on a foundation that was already crumbling.

If that's confusing, here's an example.

Picture someone genuinely successful — a bodybuilder with an incredible physique, a founder or CEO worth hundreds of millions, or someone so socially at ease they could talk to a room full of strangers without a flicker of anxiety.

Do you think the bodybuilder has to "grind" to eat clean? Does the CEO have to force themselves to show up and lead? On the surface it might look that way, but really, they can't imagine living any differently. The bodybuilder has to fight the urge to eat poorly. The CEO dreads the days they oversleep and miss the start of their routine (there's nuance here, but bear with me).

Some people find my own way of living pretty extreme. To me, it just feels normal — not because I'm trying to prove a point, but because I genuinely enjoy it. When my mom tells me to relax, get out more, and have fun, I bite my tongue instead of saying, "If I weren't enjoying this already, why would I keep doing it?"

Here's a simple idea that somehow trips up an enormous number of people:

**If you want a particular outcome, you have to live the lifestyle that produces that outcome long before you ever reach it.**

When someone tells me they want to drop 30 pounds, I'm often skeptical — not because I doubt their ability, but because that same person tends to say things like, "I can't wait until I've lost the weight so I can finally start enjoying life again." Here's the hard truth: if you don't permanently adopt the lifestyle that gets you there — and find a motivation stronger than whatever's tethering you to your old habits — you'll end up right back where you started, having burned the one resource you can't ever get back: time.

When you genuinely transform, the habits that don't serve your goal start to feel repulsive, because you now clearly see what those actions add up to over time. Right now, you tolerate your current standards simply because you haven't fully confronted what they are or where they lead. We'll get into how to uncover that — but we need to build toward it first.

You say you want to change. You say you want to be "financially free" or "get in shape." But your actions tell a different story, and the reason runs much deeper than most people realize.

## II – You're Not Where You Want to Be Because You Don't Actually Want to Be There

> Trust only movement. Life happens at the level of events, not of words. Trust movement.
> — Alfred Adler

To reshape your identity, you first need to understand how the mind operates so you can begin reprogramming it.

The starting point: all behavior serves a purpose. It's goal-driven at its core teleological, if you want the technical term. This might sound obvious once you say it out loud, but most people resist actually accepting it.

You take a step because you're heading somewhere.
You scratch an itch because you want the sensation gone.

Those examples are easy. But most of your goals are running beneath your conscious awareness. You might not notice that zoning out on the couch mid-afternoon is really an attempt to kill time before your next obligation.

Go a layer deeper, and you'll find you sometimes chase goals that actively hurt you but you dress them up in socially acceptable language so you don't come across as a failure.

For instance, if you constantly procrastinate on work, you might blame it on "lacking discipline." In reality, you're still pursuing a goal — just an unconscious one, like shielding yourself from the judgment that comes with finishing and sharing something.

If you claim you want to leave your dead-end job but never actually do, you might tell yourself you lack courage or were never really a risk-taker. But the real goal being served is safety, predictability, and a socially acceptable excuse to avoid looking like a failure to the people around you who equate steady employment with success.

The takeaway: real transformation requires transforming your goals.

I don't mean setting another surface-level target that's already been covered to death in the productivity world, and the impulse to do it often serves the very unconscious goal that's holding you back. I mean shifting your actual perspective, because that's what a goal really is: a projection into the future that filters what information, ideas, and resources you're even capable of noticing.

Let's go one level deeper, because without this, escaping the cycle only gets harder.

## III – You're Not Where You Want to Be Because You're Afraid to Be There

> The important thing for you to remember is that it does not matter in the least how you got the idea or where it came from... if you have accepted an idea... and further, if you are firmly convinced that idea is true, it has the same power over you as the hypnotist's words have over the hypnotized subject.
> — Maxwell Maltz

Here's how you became who you are and how you'll become whoever comes next. Call it the anatomy of identity:

1. You form a goal
2. You start perceiving reality through that goal's lens
3. You only register information that helps you reach it (this is learning)
4. You take action and get feedback on your progress
5. You repeat the behavior until it runs on autopilot (conditioning)
6. That behavior becomes part of your self-concept ("I'm the type of person who...")
7. You defend that identity to stay psychologically consistent
8. Your identity generates new goals, and the loop starts again which becomes a real problem if that identity isn't serving you

The hard part is breaking the loop between steps six and seven and this pattern starts forming in childhood.

As a kid, your primary goal is survival. You depend entirely on your parents to teach you how to do that. So you conform. And because most parenting relies on reward and punishment, you absorb their beliefs and values or risk being punished. Genuine independent thought doesn't happen until you see through this dynamic.

But your parents went through the exact same process. That's where things get tricky unless they consciously broke the pattern, they were shaped by whatever ideas of success were culturally dominant in their era, layered on top of whatever their own parents (and grandparents) passed down.

Go one layer further: once your physical survival is secure (which is fairly easy in the modern world — most of us are born into relative safety), you start "surviving" on a conceptual or ideological level instead. You're not defending your body anymore; you're defending your mind. Just look at the ongoing battles over ideas online most participants are really defending individual or group identities.

Threaten someone's body, and they go into fight-or-flight. Threaten someone's identity, and the exact same response fires.

If you're deeply attached to a political ideology, challenging it produces genuine stress an emotional reaction almost like being struck. And because most people never examine their emotions for accuracy, they retreat into echo chambers and dig in on positions that hurt both themselves and others.

The same applies to religious upbringing without independent reflection, or unconsciously clinging to an identity lawyer, gamer, whatever that keeps you from taking the steps toward a better life.

## IV – The Life You Want Exists at a Certain Level of Mind

The mind moves through predictable developmental stages.

At birth, you're essentially a survival sponge, soaking up whatever beliefs your culture hands you in order to feel safe. Left unchecked, your mind can harden around those early beliefs, making a meaningful life much harder to build.

This has been mapped extensively through frameworks like Maslow's Hierarchy, Loevinger's stages of ego development, Spiral Dynamics, and Integral Theory each one building on the last and it's not hard to spot in everyday life either.

I've referenced these frameworks before and synthesized them into what I call the Human 3.0 model (with tools to help you locate your own stage and next steps). Here's a condensed refresher on the nine stages of ego development, since repetition often surfaces things you missed the first time:

- **Impulsive** — No gap between urge and action; thinking is black-and-white. A toddler hits when angry because feeling and behavior are one and the same.
- **Self-Protective** — The world feels dangerous, so you learn to protect yourself. A child hides bad grades or lies about chores, learning to say what adults want to hear.
- **Conformist** — Your identity merges with your group, and its rules feel like objective reality. Someone who can't fathom why anyone would vote differently than their family.
- **Self-Aware** — You start noticing a gap between your inner experience and your outward behavior. Sitting in church, unsure you believe what everyone else seems to, but not sure what to do about it.
- **Conscientious** — You build a personal code of principles and hold yourself to it. Leaving inherited religion after real reflection, or setting a career plan because you trust effort produces results.
- **Individualist** — You realize your principles were shaped by circumstance, so you hold them more loosely. Recognizing your politics owe more to geography than objective truth, or that your career ambition was really about your father's approval.
- **Strategist** — You engage with systems while staying aware of your own role and blind spots within them. Leading an organization while actively questioning your own biases.
- **Construct-Aware** — You recognize all frameworks including your own identity as useful fictions. Holding spiritual beliefs metaphorically, or watching yourself perform the role of "founder" with quiet amusement.
- **Unitive** — The line between self and life dissolves. Work, rest, and play all blur together; there's no one left needing to "become" anything — just presence responding to whatever arises.

Most people reading this probably sit somewhere between stages four and eight a massive range. Those closer to eight are likely here for curiosity or a low-stakes way to pass time. Those closer to four are genuinely craving change, sensing they're capable of more but unable yet to make full sense of it understandably, since there's a lot going on beneath the surface.

The good news: no matter your current stage, the process for moving through it follows the same pattern.

## V – Intelligence Is the Ability to Get What You Want Out of Life

> The only real test of intelligence is if you get what you want out of life.
> — Naval Ravikant

There's a formula behind success.

One ingredient is agency.
One is opportunity (which people often misidentify as "privilege" simply because they lack the other ingredients).
The last is intelligence.

High agency without opportunity means your effort has nowhere productive to go. Opportunity and agency without intelligence means you can't fully capitalize on what's in front of you.

We've covered agency before. As for opportunity, I can't relocate you physically, but if you're not seeing the sheer volume of digital opportunity sitting right in front of you, that's on you.

So let's zoom in on intelligence, in the context of these other two ingredients. For that, we turn to cybernetics.

The word comes from the Greek *kybernetikos* means "to steer" or "skilled at steering." It's sometimes called "the art of getting what you want."

If Naval's definition of intelligence is getting what you want from life, understanding cybernetics accelerates that process considerably.

Cybernetics describes the traits of intelligent systems:

- Have a goal
- Act toward it
- Sense your current position
- Compare that position to the goal
- Adjust and act again based on that feedback

You can measure intelligence by a system's ability to persist and iterate through trial and error a ship correcting course after being blown off track, a thermostat responding to temperature shifts, a pancreas releasing insulin after a blood sugar spike.

What does that have to do with getting what you want in life? Everything.

Acting, sensing, comparing, and stepping back to see the whole system is the essence of high intelligence, under this definition. High intelligence means persisting, iterating, and seeing the big picture. Low intelligence shows up as an inability to learn from mistakes.

Less intelligent people get stuck rather than problem-solving their way through obstacles. They hit resistance and give up like a writer who fails to grow an audience and quits, instead of experimenting until they find a process that actually works (the belief that no effective process exists is simply false, regardless of your excuses which is exactly why that mindset reflects lower intelligence).

Real intelligence recognizes that any problem is solvable given enough time. You can, in fact, achieve virtually any goal you set.

Intelligence means understanding that a sequence of smaller choices leads to the larger goal that ideas build hierarchically, and you can't leap from papyrus to Google Docs in a single step. Even something that seems impossible today might just be waiting on resources that haven't been invented yet.

When I talk about "goals" here, I'm not using the typical self-help framing (though that lens has its uses). I'm speaking from a teleological standpoint — the Greek *kosmos* the idea that everything serves a purpose and belongs to something larger.

Your goals shape how you see the world. They define what counts as success or failure for you. You can try to "enjoy the journey," but chasing the wrong goal will make that impossible.

Your mind functions like the operating system running your reality and that system is built from goals.

For most people, those goals were handed to them, installed like lines of code: go to school, get the job, get offended, play the victim, retire at 65. A well-worn path that doesn't actually deliver.

To grow more intelligent, you have to:

- Reject the well-worn path
- Step into the unknown
- Set bigger, more expansive goals
- Embrace uncertainty as fuel for growth
- Study nature's underlying principles
- Become a genuine generalist

This may not match the textbook definition of intelligence, but following that sequence builds an extraordinary density of connections in your thinking — which, from the outside, reads as intelligence. Combine that with agency, and you've got a real edge.

Which brings us neatly to the next part.

## VI – How to Launch a Completely New Life (In One Day)

The best chapters of my life always followed a period of being completely fed up with my own lack of progress.

How do you actually dig into your own mind? How do you become aware of your own conditioning? How do you reach the kind of insight that shifts your entire trajectory?

Through the simple but often uncomfortable practice of questioning.

It's something remarkably few people actually do, and you can tell by how shallow most people's opinions are on any given topic. Questioning is thinking, and most people skip it entirely.

Here's a full protocol you can run once a year to reset and launch into a season of real momentum. It's built around asking the right questions — moving from the macro (where you want to end up) to the micro (what you can act on immediately).

Set aside a full day for this. Grab a pen, paper, and an open mind.

Watching people successfully reinvent themselves, I've noticed the shift tends to happen fast, after tension has been building for a while typically across three phases:

1. **Dissonance** — A growing sense that you don't belong in your current life, paired with genuine frustration at your lack of progress.
2. **Uncertainty** — Not knowing what's next, which either sparks experimentation or leaves you feeling lost and worse off.
3. **Discovery** — Finding the thing you actually want to pursue, and compressing years of progress into months.

This protocol is designed to walk you through dissonance, help you navigate the uncertainty, and land you in genuine discovery clear enough that distractions stop having any pull on you.

It's structured across a single day: a morning session digging into your hidden motives, daytime check-ins to interrupt autopilot, and an evening session where you turn everything into a concrete direction for tomorrow.

I can't promise this lands the same way for everyone, it depends on whether you're in the right chapter of your own story for these questions to actually hit. You can't put the climax at the beginning of the book and expect it to work.

### Part 1: Morning — Psychological Excavation (Vision & Anti-Vision)

First, we build a new lens for your mind to operate through like growing into a new shell before you've left the old one. It won't feel natural right away. That's expected.

Spend 15–30 minutes (about the length of a YouTube video) answering these. Don't outsource this thinking to AI the point is to push past your own mental limiter. If an answer doesn't come immediately, sit with it and return later.

- What's the dull, ongoing dissatisfaction you've learned to just live with — not the acute pain, but what you've quietly accepted?
- What do you complain about repeatedly but never change? Name your three most frequent complaints from the past year.
- For each one: watching your actions rather than listening to your words, what would an outside observer conclude you actually want?
- What truth about your current life would be unbearable to say out loud to someone you deeply respect?

Those questions surface the pain. Now we turn that into an "anti-vision". A clear-eyed picture of the life you refuse to live, so that discomfort can fuel forward motion instead of paralysis.

- If nothing changes for five years, describe an average Tuesday in detail: where you wake up, how your body feels, what crosses your mind first, who's around, what fills 9am–6pm, how you feel by 10pm.
- Now do the same for ten years out. What have you missed? What doors closed? Who stopped believing in you? What do people say about you when you're not around?
- Now imagine the end of your life, having played it safe the whole way. What did that cost you? What did you never let yourself feel, try, or become?
- Who's already living the future you just described five, ten, twenty years further down that same road? What comes up when you picture becoming them?
- What identity would you have to let go of to actually change ("I'm the type of person who...")? What would that cost you socially?
- What's the most embarrassing real reason you haven't changed yet — the one that sounds weak or scared rather than reasonable?
- If your current behavior is really a form of self-protection, what exactly is it protecting and what's that protection costing you?

If you answered honestly, and you're in the right season of life for this, you should feel real discomfort maybe even disgust about your current trajectory. Now we redirect that energy. Think of your vision as a product: rough and unclear at first, sharpening with time.

- Forgetting practicality entirely — if you could snap your fingers into a different life three years from now, what would it actually look like? Same level of detail as before, describing an average Tuesday.
- What would you need to believe about yourself for that life to feel natural instead of forced? Write the identity statement: "I am the type of person who..."
- What's one thing you'd do this week if you were already that person?

Answer all of this first thing tomorrow morning.

### Part 2: Throughout the Day — Interrupting Autopilot

Journaling alone is nice, but real change needs more than that it requires actively breaking your current unconscious patterns.

Throughout the day, keep circling back to what you wrote this morning. Beyond that, don't let yourself forget to keep questioning. Take this seriously repeating the same day on loop won't produce change. You need to force a deliberate break in the pattern.

Right now, set reminders or calendar events on your phone with these questions embedded directly in them. The more randomly they're scattered through your day (without clashing with your schedule), the better:

- 11:00am: What am I avoiding right now by doing what I'm doing?
- 1:30pm: If someone filmed the last two hours, what would they conclude I actually want from life?
- 3:15pm: Am I moving toward the life I hate, or the life I want?
- 5:00pm: What's the most important thing I'm pretending isn't important?
- 7:30pm: What did I do today out of protecting my identity rather than genuine desire? (Hint: probably most of it.)
- 9:00pm: When did I feel most alive today? Most dead?

For extra impact, schedule these during commutes, walks, or downtime:

- What would shift if I stopped needing people to see me as [the identity from question ten]?
- Where in my life am I trading aliveness for safety?
- What's the smallest version of who I want to become that I could be tomorrow?

### Part 3: Evening — Synthesizing Insight

If you followed through, you likely surfaced at least one insight capable of shifting your trajectory. Now it's time to name it, absorb it, and act on it.

- After today, what feels most true about why you've been stuck?
- Name the actual enemy clearly — not circumstances, not other people, but the internal belief or pattern that's really been running things.
- Write one sentence capturing what you refuse to let your life become — your anti-vision, compressed. It should hit you emotionally.
- Write one sentence capturing what you're building toward, understanding it will keep evolving — your vision, in its earliest form.

Finally, set your goals — not as fixed finish lines (which don't really exist), but as shifting lenses that put you in the right frame of mind to act:

- **One-year lens:** What would have to be true a year from now for you to know you broke the old pattern? Name one concrete thing.
- **One-month lens:** What needs to be true a month from now to keep that one-year outcome realistic?
- **Daily lens:** What two or three actions could you block off tomorrow that the person you're becoming would simply do without thinking twice?

That was a lot to get through — hopefully useful. One more piece left to lock it all in.

## VII – Turn Your Life Into a Video Game

> The optimal state of inner experience is one in which there is order in consciousness. This happens when psychic energy—or attention—is invested in realistic goals, and when skills match the opportunities for action. The pursuit of a goal brings order in awareness because a person must concentrate attention on the task at hand and momentarily forget everything else.
> — Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi

You now have every piece needed to build a good life. It might help to organize your insights into a single, coherent structure. On a fresh page, write down these six elements:

1. **Anti-vision** — the life you never want to live again
2. **Vision** — the life you're aiming for, one you can refine as you go
3. **One-year goal** — what your life should look like a year from now, and whether that's actually closer to what you want
4. **One-month project** — what you need to learn, build, or acquire to move toward that year-long goal
5. **Daily levers** — the highest-priority actions that push the project forward
6. **Constraints** — the things you refuse to sacrifice along the way

Why does this matter so much? Because these six pieces effectively construct your own private world. If this is genuinely the right stage of life for this hierarchy of goals, obsession becomes almost automatic. You'll feel pulled toward something bigger, with no real appetite for anything else.

You're essentially turning your life into a video game — because games are the gold standard for focus, enjoyment, and flow. If we reverse-engineer what makes games so gripping, we can borrow those same mechanics for real life.

- Your **vision** is how you win (until the game evolves).
- Your **anti-vision** is what's at stake if you lose or quit.
- Your **one-year goal** is the main mission — your top priority.
- Your **one-month project** is the boss fight — where you earn XP and loot.
- Your **daily levers** are the quests — the routine that unlocks new opportunities.
- Your **constraints** are the rules — limitations that actually spark creativity.

Together, these form concentric rings — a kind of forcefield protecting your focus from distraction. The more you engage with this system, the stronger it becomes, until eventually it simply becomes who you are — and you wouldn't want it any other way.
